What Is a Trading Broker and How Do They Make Money?

The middleman between you and the market — and the business model behind the app on your phone.

6 min read Money Moves India Last updated: 14 Aug 2026

Every trade you place goes through a broker — the platform that connects you to the actual stock exchange or market. Understanding what a broker actually does, and how they make their money, helps explain why not all brokers are the same and why the choice actually matters.

What a broker actually does

Individual traders can't directly place orders on an exchange — brokers are the licensed intermediaries that route your buy and sell orders to the exchange, hold your holdings in a demat account, and provide the app or platform you actually use to place trades. Without a broker, there's no practical way for an individual to access the market directly.

How brokers make money: brokerage fees

The most direct way brokers earn is a brokerage fee charged per trade — sometimes a flat fee, sometimes a percentage of the trade value, and increasingly, especially among newer discount brokers, sometimes zero for certain types of trades like equity delivery. This fee structure varies enough between brokers that it's worth comparing directly if you trade frequently, since the difference compounds over many trades.

Other, less obvious revenue sources

Beyond direct brokerage, many brokers also earn from account maintenance charges (a small recurring fee for holding your demat account), interest on funds sitting idle in your trading account, charges for specific services like margin trading, and sometimes commissions from third parties for promoting other financial products through their platform. None of this is hidden exactly, but it's rarely emphasised upfront either.

Discount brokers versus full-service brokers

Discount brokers offer a straightforward, low-cost platform focused purely on placing trades, with minimal advisory support — you're expected to make your own decisions. Full-service brokers charge more but typically bundle in research reports, dedicated advisors, and more hand-holding. Neither is objectively better; the right choice depends on whether you want to research and decide independently or want more guidance, at a cost.

What actually matters when comparing brokers

Beyond the headline brokerage rate, it's worth checking the platform's reliability (does it crash during high-volatility periods, when you most need it to work), the quality of customer support, and how transparent their full fee structure is — not just the number they advertise most prominently. A slightly higher brokerage fee on a platform that's reliable and easy to use is often a better trade-off than the cheapest option that frequently has technical issues.

Checking that a broker is actually regulated

In India, a legitimate stock broker should be registered with SEBI (the Securities and Exchange Board of India) and be a member of the exchanges it lets you trade on, such as the NSE or BSE. This registration is checkable directly on SEBI's own website before you open an account anywhere. Skipping this check and judging a broker purely by app reviews or social media promotion is how people end up on unregulated platforms that offer no real protection if something goes wrong with your funds or holdings.

Opening your first account as a minor

Demat and trading accounts in India generally require the account holder to be 18 or older, so if you're not yet an adult, you can't open one directly in your own name. The common path is a parent or guardian opening an account and either trading on your behalf with your input, or setting up a minor's demat account specifically designed for this, which comes with its own rules about who can operate it. This is worth discussing directly with a parent and the broker rather than assuming any one path applies to your situation.

Good to knowRead a broker's full fee schedule, not just the advertised brokerage rate — account maintenance and other charges can add up meaningfully over a year even if you rarely trade.
