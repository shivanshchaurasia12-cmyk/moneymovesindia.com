Understanding Currency Pairs: Majors, Minors & Exotics

Not all currency pairs behave the same way — here's how traders group them and why it matters.

6 min read Money Moves India Last updated: 09 Aug 2026

Currency pairs can look confusing at first — two three-letter codes stacked together with a price attached — but the underlying idea is simple once broken down. Here's what majors, minors, and exotics actually mean, and why the distinction matters.

The basic idea behind a currency pair

Every currency pair expresses the value of one currency relative to another. USD/INR, for example, tells you how many rupees it takes to buy one US dollar. When you "trade" a currency pair, you're effectively betting on whether the first currency will strengthen or weaken against the second one — there's no third option, since currencies only ever move relative to each other.

Major pairs

Major pairs always include the US dollar paired with another heavily traded global currency, such as the euro, the British pound, or the Japanese yen. These pairs have the highest trading volume in the world, which generally means tighter spreads (the gap between buy and sell price) and more predictable behaviour, since so many participants are constantly trading them.

Minor pairs

Minor pairs (sometimes called "crosses") involve two major currencies but exclude the US dollar entirely — a euro-to-pound pair, for instance. They're still reasonably liquid and widely traded, just with somewhat lower volume than major pairs, which can mean slightly wider spreads and marginally less predictable short-term movement.

Exotic pairs

Exotic pairs combine a major currency with a currency from a smaller or emerging economy. USD/INR itself is generally considered an exotic pair. These pairs typically have lower trading volume, wider spreads, and can move more sharply on local economic news specific to that smaller economy — which makes them riskier and less predictable for a beginner to trade compared to major pairs.

Why this distinction actually matters for a beginner

Wider spreads on minor and exotic pairs mean a larger built-in cost on every single trade before you've even made or lost money on the price movement itself. For someone learning the mechanics of currency trading, starting with major pairs — where costs are lower and behaviour tends to be more consistent — generally makes for a gentler, less expensive learning curve than jumping straight into exotic pairs.

Reading a currency pair's price

When you see USD/INR quoted, the first currency listed is the "base" and the second is the "quote" — the price tells you how much of the quote currency it takes to buy one unit of the base. Getting comfortable reading pairs this way, rather than memorising each pair separately, makes it much easier to understand any new pair you come across later.

What actually moves currency pair prices

Currency values shift constantly based on a mix of factors: a country's interest rate decisions, inflation data, political stability, and overall economic growth all play a role, alongside pure supply and demand from traders, businesses, and governments buying or selling that currency for their own reasons. For a pair like USD/INR specifically, India's trade balance, foreign investment flows, and RBI policy decisions tend to matter alongside whatever is happening with the US dollar globally. None of this needs to be memorised in detail as a beginner — the useful takeaway is simply that price movement has real underlying causes, not random noise, even when a short-term chart looks erratic.

Keep in mindA pair's classification as major, minor, or exotic is really just a shorthand for how liquid and how volatile it tends to be — check both before trading any pair you're unfamiliar with.
