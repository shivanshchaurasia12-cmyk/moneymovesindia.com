Budgeting Apps in India: A Practical Comparison

What actually differs between the popular budgeting apps beyond the marketing.

6 min read Money Moves India Updated 2026

Most students don't need a complicated finance app — they need something that shows where money actually went last month, without a 20-minute setup process. Here's how the common categories of budgeting apps used in India actually compare in practice, not just on paper.

SMS/UPI auto-tracking apps

These apps read your transaction SMS or connect to your UPI apps and build your spending list automatically, with no manual entry required. The upside is obvious — you open the app and your last week of spending is already there. The downside is they can miscategorise transactions (a UPI payment to a friend might get logged as a random category) and they need SMS or account access, which some students are understandably cautious about granting.

Manual-entry expense trackers

Simpler apps where you type in every expense by hand. They're lighter, ask for less access to your phone, and force a small moment of awareness every time you spend — which some people find actually helps them spend less. The catch is consistency: manual trackers only work if you actually open the app and log the coffee, the auto fare, the recharge. Most people stop within two weeks unless the habit is deliberately built in.

Spreadsheet-based tracking

A simple Google Sheet with columns for date, category, and amount costs nothing, can't leak your data anywhere, and can be customised exactly how you want. It takes more discipline to maintain than an app with reminders, but for a student just trying to understand three or four broad categories — food, transport, subscriptions, "other" — it's often more than enough, and it's a skill that carries over into every other spreadsheet you'll use later.

What actually matters more than the app

The single biggest factor in whether budget tracking works isn't which app you pick — it's whether you review it. An app that auto-logs everything but that you never open is worthless. A basic spreadsheet you check every Sunday for five minutes will teach you more about your spending patterns than any feature-rich app you ignore.

A simple way to start

Pick one method, commit to using it for two weeks only, and check it every few days. If it's a manual tracker and you stop logging entries after day three, that's useful information too — it tells you to switch to something more automatic rather than trying to force willpower to fix a tool problem.

What to check before granting app permissions

Auto-tracking apps ask for SMS or UPI access, and it's worth pausing on that before tapping "allow" on the first screen you see. Look for whether the app is listed on the Play Store or App Store with a reasonable number of reviews and an actual company behind it, not just a name. Read the permissions screen instead of skipping past it — an app that only needs SMS read-access to log transactions is different from one asking for contacts, location, and camera access for no clear reason. If a free app is asking for more than it needs to do its one job, that's worth treating as a red flag rather than an inconvenience.

Common mistakes students make with these apps

The most common one is installing three tracking apps at once, splitting spending data across all of them, and ending up with a less clear picture than before. A second is treating the app as the goal rather than the habit — redesigning categories and tweaking settings for an hour instead of just logging five real transactions. A third is giving up the first time a category gets miscategorised, instead of just fixing that one entry and moving on. None of these mistakes are really about the app; they're about expecting the tool to do the discipline part for you.

Worth knowingAny tracking method beats no tracking method. Don't spend more time comparing apps than you'd spend just starting with the simplest one and adjusting later.
