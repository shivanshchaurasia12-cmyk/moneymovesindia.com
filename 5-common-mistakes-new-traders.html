5 Common Mistakes New Traders Make

The patterns that quietly wipe out beginner accounts — and how to notice them in yourself before they do.

7 min read Money Moves India Updated 2026

Most new traders in India don't blow up their account because of bad luck. They blow it up because of a small set of mistakes that repeat across almost every beginner's journey — the same five, in roughly the same order. Knowing them in advance won't make you immune, but it will make them easier to catch before they get expensive.

1. Trading without a written plan

"I'll enter if it looks good" is not a plan — it's a guess dressed up as a strategy. A real plan answers three questions before you place a single trade: what makes this a valid entry, where exactly does it become wrong (your stop-loss), and where do you take profit. Write these down for every trade, even a one-line note. If you can't answer all three, you're not trading yet — you're gambling with extra steps.

2. Risking too much on a single trade

A common beginner habit is sizing a position based on conviction — "I'm really sure about this one" — instead of sizing it based on account risk. Professional traders typically risk 1-2% of their total capital on any single trade, regardless of how confident they feel. This means even a string of five or six losing trades in a row barely dents the account, and no single mistake can wipe you out. Beginners who ignore this often survive their first few trades on luck, then lose it all on the one they were "sure about."

3. Moving the stop-loss when the trade goes wrong

This is the mistake that turns a small, manageable loss into a account-threatening one. The trade moves against you, and instead of accepting the small loss you'd already planned for, you widen the stop-loss "just to give it more room." Nine times out of ten this doesn't save the trade — it just makes the eventual loss bigger. A stop-loss you're willing to move isn't really a stop-loss.

4. Copying trades from social media and Telegram groups

Following someone else's buy/sell call without understanding why they took it means you have no idea when to exit, no sense of the risk involved, and no way to tell a genuine analyst from someone pumping a position they already hold. If you can't explain a trade in your own words, it isn't your trade — it's someone else's bet with your money attached.

5. Trying to "make it back" immediately after a loss

After a loss, the instinct is to jump straight into another trade to recover the money fast. This is usually when judgement is at its worst — rushed, emotional, and looking for any excuse to enter. The traders who last are the ones who can walk away after a bad trade, review what happened calmly, and come back later with a clear head rather than an urge to get even with the market.

Worth knowingEvery one of these mistakes is avoidable with a simple rule: decide your entry, stop-loss, and position size before you open the trade — not while you're already in it.
