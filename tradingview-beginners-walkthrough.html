TradingView for Beginners: A Walkthrough

One of the most widely used charting tools — and how to make sense of it on your first visit.

7 min read Money Moves India Updated 2026

TradingView is one of the most widely used charting platforms among retail traders, largely because its free tier already covers what most beginners need. Here's a practical walkthrough of what to actually do when you open it for the first time.

Setting up your first chart

After creating a free account, searching for a symbol — a stock ticker, an index, a currency pair — pulls up its price chart immediately. The default candlestick view shows price movement over your selected timeframe, with each candle representing one unit of time (a minute, an hour, a day, depending on what you select from the timeframe menu near the top).

Understanding the timeframe selector

The timeframe you choose changes what the chart is actually telling you — a 5-minute chart shows short-term price noise useful for very short-term trading, while a daily or weekly chart smooths that out and shows the broader trend. A common beginner mistake is analysing a short timeframe while thinking about a long-term decision, or vice versa; matching your timeframe to your actual trading horizon matters more than beginners initially expect.

Adding basic indicators

The "Indicators" button lets you add tools like moving averages, RSI, or volume directly onto your chart. As a beginner, resist the urge to add many indicators at once — a cluttered chart with ten overlapping indicators is harder to read, not easier. Learning to interpret one or two well is more useful than a chart covered in signals you don't fully understand.

Drawing tools worth learning first

The trendline and horizontal line tools, both easily accessible from the left toolbar, are the most fundamental drawing tools and worth learning before anything more advanced. A horizontal line at a price level that's acted as support or resistance in the past, or a trendline connecting a series of highs or lows, gives a visual sense of where price has previously reacted — a genuinely useful starting skill.

Saving your setup and watchlists

Once you've set up a chart layout you like, save it so you don't have to rebuild it every time. Building a watchlist of assets you follow regularly — accessible from the panel on the right — means you can check on your usual set of instruments in seconds instead of searching for each one individually every session.

Using alerts instead of watching charts all day

Right-clicking on a price level or an indicator lets you set an alert that notifies you when price reaches that point, which removes the need to sit staring at a chart waiting for something to happen. This is one of the more underused features by beginners, who often develop a habit of constantly refreshing charts out of anxiety rather than setting a clear alert and doing something else until it triggers. Alerts also enforce a useful discipline: deciding in advance what price level actually matters to you, rather than reacting emotionally to every small movement as it happens.

A simple first-week routine

Rather than trying to learn every feature at once, a manageable first week looks like: day one, set up your account and add five or six symbols you're genuinely interested in to a watchlist; the next few days, practice drawing trendlines and horizontal support/resistance lines on those charts without adding any indicators yet; toward the end of the week, add just one indicator, like a simple moving average, and watch how it behaves against price you're now familiar with. This slower pace builds real chart-reading intuition instead of a shallow familiarity with a dozen tools at once.

Worth knowingThe free tier of TradingView covers everything a beginner needs to start learning to read charts properly — there's no need to pay for a subscription until you have a specific, advanced feature you actually require.
